Report Description

This report presents a statistical analysis and hypothesis testing project completed as part of Week 3 of the Virtual Data Science with Python apprenticeship. The objective was to apply rigorous statistical methods in Python to test a clearly defined, real-world-relevant hypothesis: whether the amount of time students spend studying each day is associated with a meaningful difference in their exam performance.

For this analysis, a self-generated educational dataset of 120 student records was created, containing each student's daily study hours, attendance percentage, and exam score. Students were divided into two groups based on a 5-hour daily study threshold: those studying 5 or more hours per day (64 students) and those studying fewer than 5 hours per day (56 students). The research question investigated was whether the average exam score differs significantly between these two groups, or whether any observed difference could reasonably be attributed to random variation.

The analysis began with descriptive statistics for each group, followed by formal hypothesis formulation: a null hypothesis stating that there is no significant difference in mean exam scores between the two groups, and an alternative hypothesis stating that a significant difference exists. Levene's test was first used to check the equality of variances between groups, and Welch's independent-samples t-test was then applied, since it does not require the assumption of equal variances. The test produced a highly significant result (t = 7.476, p ≈ 1.97 × 10⁻¹¹), leading to rejection of the null hypothesis. A 95% confidence interval for the difference in means, calculated using the Welch–Satterthwaite approach, further confirmed that students studying 5+ hours per day scored, on average, between roughly 6.8 and 11.7 points higher. Histograms, a box plot, and a scatter plot were created to visually support these findings.

The report documents the full workflow — from dataset generation and descriptive statistics through hypothesis testing, confidence interval estimation, and visualization — using Python libraries including Pandas, NumPy, SciPy, and Matplotlib. It concludes that, within this self-generated dataset, daily study time is strongly associated with exam performance, while also discussing the limitations of using simulated data and the appropriate scope of these conclusions.
